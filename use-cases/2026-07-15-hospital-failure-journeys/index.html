Among low-rated Google reviews of U.S. hospitals and other healthcare facilities, almost half of those mentioning insurance also mention billing. A concern routed only as an insurance issue could therefore lose the charge-related context needed to understand it. Looking at linked topics helps a hospital decide what to investigate together and which people or records to involve.

The same principle leads to different questions elsewhere: waiting alongside reported capacity constraints calls for an operational check; behavioral health alongside perceived unfair treatment calls for close examination of the described interaction. Counting each topic separately can identify priorities, but it cannot show this shared context.

These findings come from 72,038 labeled one- and two-star reviews of U.S. healthcare listings, including hospitals, clinics and urgent-care services, from 2019 to early June 2026. They describe self-selected public feedback, not measured incident rates. The labels have passed automated checks; human validation remains pending. A connection means two topics occur in the same account, without establishing a single visit, event order or cause.

Financial concerns often need both charge and coverage context

Billing and insurance occur together in 908 low-rated reviews, about 4.6 times the shuffled comparison’s expected count of 199. More useful for follow-up is the asymmetry: 46% of insurance-related accounts also mention billing, while 12% of billing-related accounts mention insurance.

The same 908 accounts sit behind both percentages. They answer different questions about context, not which problem came first. Coverage concerns often come with a description of charges; most charge-related feedback does not mention coverage. A hospital should therefore avoid treating every billing dispute as an insurance problem, while checking for financial context when examining coverage feedback.

This pairing largely reflects concerns about both subjects: 843 of the 908 accounts, or 93%, contain a negative statement about each. That check matters because a low overall rating does not make every topic mentioned a complaint.

The two categories also cover different issues. Billing includes unexpected or disputed charges, payment and collections; insurance includes coverage verification, denials, network and authorization. Reading the joint accounts can distinguish what was charged, what coverage decision was described, and what explanation was given. Those distinctions determine whether financial-services records, coverage or authorization records, or both are relevant.

For a hospital team, the practical lesson is to keep the charge and coverage descriptions together during follow-up. An accurate charge could still be poorly explained; a described coverage dispute needs different evidence from an alleged duplicate bill. These are questions to test against the full account and available records. The aggregate pairing does not identify which explanation applies or demonstrate a handoff failure.

Waiting feedback calls for different operational questions

Waiting and staffing/operational capacity appear together in 2,075 low-rated reviews, about 1.36 times the shuffled expectation. Waiting is mentioned in 47% of capacity-related feedback, but capacity appears in only 9% of waiting feedback. These shares use 4,398 capacity-related and 22,622 waiting-related reviews as their respective denominators.

The distinction changes the question a hospital should ask. Capacity concerns often come with delays, but most waiting feedback does not explicitly describe a capacity constraint. The “staffing” label in the charts covers beds, equipment and overcrowding as well as personnel. It measures what people describe, rather than actual staffing levels, and cannot justify a general recommendation to hire more staff.

In 2,042 of the 2,075 paired accounts, or 98%, both topics have a negative statement. This gives an operations team a focused set of jointly described concerns to examine. The useful next step is to locate the waiting stage and the reported constraint: arrival, testing, admission or another part of care. Available demand, resource and stage-specific waiting records can then help assess whether that description fits an operational bottleneck. Explanations given during the wait belong in the same reading, since a reported constraint and uncertainty about a delay require different follow-up.

Administrative experiences offer a useful contrast. Front-desk conduct and waiting appear together in 4,436 low-rated reviews, more than twice the capacity/waiting count. Yet this pairing is not unusually concentrated under the comparison model: its independently calculated lift is about 0.96. A large recurring set of concerns can deserve attention even when it is absent from the retained association map.

That is why volume, concentration and seriousness need separate judgments. Counts help locate a substantial body of feedback. Lift helps identify relationships that appear more often than the comparison predicts. The full account and corroborating evidence determine the significance of a particular concern. Neither a large count nor a high lift estimates the benefit of an intervention.

Smaller connected concerns deserve specific follow-up

Behavioral health and discrimination/equitable treatment appear together in 212 low-rated reviews, roughly twice the shuffled expectation. 203 of these accounts, or 96%, contain a negative statement about both topics. The lower volume does not settle how significant the described experiences may be.

The value of the pairing is a narrower reading question: how are the treatment setting and the interaction perceived as unfair described together? Among these 212 accounts, 51 also mention bedside manner, 34 medication and 32 safety. These overlapping subsets suggest additional context to check in particular accounts; they do not show that everyone describes the same set of problems.

A service or patient-experience team could use this selection to examine the reported interaction, what treatment or explanation was described, and what would need verification. Relevant concerns can then enter the appropriate existing service, experience or quality process. The model’s categories do not establish a diagnosis, the author’s role, verified discrimination or a safety incident.

The learning is to preserve the specific concern when summarizing smaller categories. A broad “staff experience” summary could obscure the question of perceived fairness. A network group cannot determine seriousness or ownership on its own; those decisions need human reading and appropriate supporting evidence.

Lift divides observed co-mentions by the average expected under shuffled topic assignments. The comparison preserves topic frequencies within groups originally mentioning two, three, four or five-plus topics. It helps account for popular topics and the greater pairing opportunities in multi-topic accounts. It does not hold word length or every shuffled row’s topic count fixed.

topicNetwork = {
  const bundleOf = new Map(topicBundles.flatMap(b => b.topics.map(t => [t, b.name])));
  const seen = new Set(), links = [];
  for (const r of pairMatrix) {
    const key = [r.a, r.b].sort().join("|");
    if (seen.has(key)) continue;
    seen.add(key);
    links.push({source: r.a, target: r.b, lift: r.lift, same: r.same_bundle});
  }
  const degree = new Map();
  links.forEach(l => { degree.set(l.source, (degree.get(l.source) || 0) + 1); degree.set(l.target, (degree.get(l.target) || 0) + 1); });
  // Start each group on its own spot around a circle so the layout is the same on every load.
  const groups = topicBundles.map(b => b.name);
  const nodes = topicOrder.map((t, i) => {
    const g = groups.indexOf(bundleOf.get(t)), a = (g / groups.length) * 2 * Math.PI;
    return {id: t, bundle: bundleOf.get(t), degree: degree.get(t) || 0, x: Math.cos(a) * 200 + (i % 3) * 8, y: Math.sin(a) * 150 + (i % 2) * 8};
  });
  const sim = d3.forceSimulation(nodes)
    .force("link", d3.forceLink(links).id(d => d.id).distance(l => l.same ? 58 : 110).strength(l => 0.25 + 0.12 * l.lift))
    .force("charge", d3.forceManyBody().strength(-430))
    .force("collide", d3.forceCollide(52))
    .force("x", d3.forceX(0).strength(0.05))
    .force("y", d3.forceY(0).strength(0.07))
    .stop();
  for (let i = 0; i < 400; i++) sim.tick();
  return {nodes, links};
}

Turn a connection into a better investigation

The practical output is a more specific question and the context needed to answer it. Start by validating the labels and reading each selected account in full. Identify the uncertainty before choosing participants or requesting records. The examples suggest different follow-up needs:

Linked concerns Context to preserve Question and evidence for follow-up
Billing with insurance Charges, coverage decisions and the explanation described What was disputed or unclear? Which financial and coverage records, and which teams, can assess it?
Waiting with capacity Waiting stage, reported resource constraint and communication Do available process, demand and capacity records support the described bottleneck, or point to a different question?
Behavioral health with equitable treatment Treatment setting, interaction and perceived unfairness What needs human verification, and which existing service, experience or quality process should receive it?

Keep the linked context attached if the concern moves between teams. If an investigation leads to a process change, assess that change using independent process and experience evidence. A shift in the number of public co-mentions alone cannot establish improvement: participation, listing mix and the language people use may also change.

The connections help choose what to examine together. They do not establish escalation over time or an extra combined rating penalty. The supporting checks below explain those limits and why topic-level sentiment must be checked even in highly rated feedback.

Supporting plots, methods and aggregate data

Scope and labels. The year- and listing-stratified coverage sample contains 217,343 reviews from 2019 to early June 2026. Of these, 196,183 have at least one label passing automated topic, quoted-phrase, review-ID and sentiment checks. Within the 72,038 labeled one- and two-star reviews, 47,405 mention at least two distinct topics. Reviews are keyed by listing plus review ID and counted once per topic. Healthcare listings are not necessarily distinct hospitals. Automated validity checks do not replace the pending human label audit, and topic labels describe reported experiences rather than verified events.

Comparison. Topic columns are independently shuffled 499 times within original topic-count strata (2, 3, 4, 5+), skipping strata below 50 reviews. This preserves each topic’s stratum-level frequency, but not exact word length or every shuffled row’s topic count. Pairs with at least 30 observed co-mentions are tested; retention requires a Benjamini–Hochberg q-value below 0.05 and lift above 1.15. Greedy modularity groups the network using log₂ lift as the weight. Group names are editorial; stability across alternative clustering algorithms is not established.

New descriptive checks. Conditional shares divide co-mentions by all labeled reviews in the rating band mentioning the starting topic. A topic has a negative or positive flag when any validity-gated statement has that sentiment; flags can overlap. Co-mentions in the original screen can have any sentiment. The focal sentiment checks support interpreting these particular low-rated pairs as joint concerns, rather than establishing that every pair is jointly negative. Exact shuffle expectations reconcile the saved retained-pair lifts to within 1.28%; the charts retain their original finite-shuffle estimates. No statistical screen or interaction model is refitted.

Preserved analyses. The earlier exploratory report retains the unadjusted severity comparison, facility-category checks and monthly and pandemic-period series. Read its unadjusted ratings alongside the adjusted model above; causal or compound-penalty interpretations are not supported by these findings. The executed notebook reports mean correlations of monthly attention changes of 0.119 within groups versus −0.001 across groups; corresponding tone correlations are 0.105 versus 0.091. These exploratory summaries do not independently validate the grouping or establish an early-warning system. Listing mix and the single pandemic period complicate interpretation.
